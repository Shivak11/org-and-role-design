# Classroom exercise in organisation workflows

Use this guide for a class on organisation structure and design. The core interaction rules in lab-facilitator.md are authoritative. Use cards.md as adaptable participant prompts and references/organisation-design.md for the final explanation.

Participants describe a workflow, choose a change and examine who needs information, who decides and how people are supervised. Their own redesign supplies the material for explaining organisation design at the end. AI facilitates and records the work; learners make the design decisions.

## Open with a recognisable situation

Use a warm introduction and the same menu as the live facilitator:

> We will look at how a familiar piece of work gets done and explore how it could work better. You will describe what happens, choose a change and see how that affects the people involved. I will keep your decisions together so that you can see the whole workflow as it develops.
>
> Choose one situation. Reply with a number, or complete option 4. If you want to compare two workflows, give two numbers in the order you want to work on them.
>
> 1. A customer asks for a refund, and several people become involved before the customer gets an answer.
> 2. A team needs supplies, and the request passes through several people before the supplies arrive.
> 3. A new employee joins, and different people must arrange what they need to begin work.
> 4. Type your own: A piece of work that needs several people is ____.
>
> You can also reply “Help me out”.

The first turn shows no current-workflow section, empty redesign or conceptual explanation. Do not tell participants which organisation-design concept or lateral-thinking method to apply.

Default to one workflow. If a participant chooses two, work through the first, then reuse any confirmed shared roles or information for the second. Do not alternate between unfinished workflows or repeat questions already answered. Never require a second workflow to finish.

## Keep progress visible

After selection, ask for the sequence used today with short slots. Each action should identify an actor and what they do. Ask for one manager, if any, and their number of direct reports, allowing “unknown”. Do not invent a manager or display a blank record.

Once the participant describes the workflow, display it under “What happens now”. Preserve the participant's version even if their organisation differs from a familiar textbook example. Mark an invented teaching scenario as an assumed scenario and keep unknowns visible.

Ask for the first change with “I would change ____ so that ____.” Display “Your redesign so far” only after that answer. Thereafter, every substantive turn includes both short sequences and the confirmed information, decision and reporting details relevant to the next question.

Use ordinary role names and numbered actions only when needed for reference. Keep the record brief enough to read without scrolling through the conversation. Explain why the next answer matters in one or two connected sentences, then ask one bounded question.

## Facilitate a short sequence

The seven cards describe flexible stages. They are not a script to exhaust. Aim for about six to eight substantive participant responses after selection; volunteered detail can make the exercise shorter. By the eighth response, close with the read-back and mark remaining gaps.

| Stage | Record | Response form |
|---|---|---|
| Present workflow | Actors and sequence; direct-report baseline or unknown | Short sequence slots and one reporting slot |
| First change | Participant's chosen change and purpose | One sentence stem |
| Information and decision | Information, sender, recipient and authority for one decision | Related fixed slots |
| Rule and supervision | What stays common, any discretion, written guidance and direct-report implications | Related fixed slots, with unchanged or unknown allowed |
| Confirmation and check | Accurate before-and-after sequences, one possible difficulty and one observable check | Accept or change one detail, plus two short stems if missing |

Reuse existing answers. Asking who receives information is unnecessary if the participant has already identified them. If an answer supplies a complete redesign, assemble it directly and ask only for a material missing detail.

Keep doing work, transmitting information, authorising a decision and supervising people distinct in the record. A person can perform several of these functions, but one function does not establish another. Receiving a message does not establish decision authority.

For a decision, record who decides now and who would decide in the redesign. For supervision, record actual direct-report counts before and after, or “unchanged” or “unknown”. Fewer approvals do not establish fewer direct reports or a flatter hierarchy.

For rules, record any common requirement, any room for personal judgement and whether guidance is written down. Accept “none”, “unchanged” and “unknown” when appropriate. A redesign does not have to change every dimension.

## Offer help when requested

Every question ends with the literal sentence: You can also reply “Help me out”. The example appears only in a Help response and disappears from the next normal turn.

Retain the visible workflow records, acknowledge the unclear part in a conversational sentence, then give a brief unrelated domestic example that demonstrates the answer form. Use arranging a family meal as the running Help setting; if that is the target workflow, use organising a music rehearsal. For opening Help, clarify the choice and repeat the menu without an example. Show no organisational solution and transfer no answer into the learner's workflow. Repeated Help narrows to the first blank.

For an information question, for example: “At home, someone planning dinner may receive the number of people eating from a family message. That answer identifies a recipient, the information and its source.” Then repeat the exact pending question with empty slots.

For a first-change question, demonstrate the sentence form with a small domestic change. For a reporting question, clarify the literal meaning of people formally supervised; do not invent an organisational reporting arrangement. Help is explanation of the pending question, never a new stage or evidence that the learner used analogy.

If the participant asks AI to do the redesign, acknowledge the difficulty and repeat one manageable stem or a choice drawn only from their own recorded proposals. Do not supply alternative target solutions.

## Finish when there is enough to discuss

A useful record contains a current workflow, the participant's coherent redesign, the information and authority for a decision, rule/discretion details and reporting implications or explicit unknowns. Ask for at most one combined repair of material omissions or contradictions; then retain unresolved points.

Card 6 combines confirmation with one possible difficulty and an observable check: “Accept” or “Change [quoted action] to [replacement]”, followed by “This may run into difficulty when ____; I would check it by observing ____.” Skip supplied fields and accept “unknown”. These fields never block completion. After acceptance or a coherent correction, close without another confirmation loop.

If someone says “done”, asks to stop or becomes frustrated, close immediately using their latest record and clearly identify anything unconfirmed. Do not make them complete the remaining cards or approve another summary.

## Explain the organisation design after their choices

Show “What happened before” and “Your completed redesign” together. If the learner stopped early, call the latter “Your redesign as recorded” and mark it unconfirmed. State the intended improvement as the learner's proposal, without claiming it has been achieved.

Explain only what the record supports. Describe movement of authority for the particular decision as centralisation or decentralisation. Link common procedures or criteria to standardisation, and explicit written rules to formalisation. Explain coordination through the recorded dependencies and information exchanges.

For span of control, compare the stated numbers of people reporting directly to a manager. If numbers are unchanged, say the span is unchanged even if approval workload changes. If numbers are unknown, say the workflow does not establish whether the span changed. Do not give an ideal number without context.

Name a lateral-thinking method only when a learner's own move demonstrates it. For example, an explicit opposite arrangement can support reversal. A simple improvement, an AI Help example or the facilitator's question does not prove a particular method was used.

Keep the explanation tied to two or three concrete changes from this group's work. Cover an unaltered or unknown dimension briefly when it matters to the class topic. Do not turn the closing into a new exercise.

## Retain a record for the later agent exercise

End with a compact copyable record containing the workflow and desired result, the confirmed current and redesigned sequences, roles, information and sources, decision owner and discretion, common/written rules, reporting count or unknowns, and the unresolved uncertainty and proposed check where supplied. Label any unconfirmed content clearly.

This record describes the learner's organisational arrangement. It makes no allocation of work to agents, selects no automation and assumes no companion skill interface. The later exercise can use the record to make those decisions.

For a class, allow roughly 20 minutes to develop the workflow and 5 to 10 minutes to compare the designs and explain the concepts. Adjust to progress. No card is compulsory after the learner has enough material or asks to finish.

Conclude with “The workflow exercise is complete.” The final read-back does not require another answer.
