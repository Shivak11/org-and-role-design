# Organisation design through a workflow

Use this reference to interpret the learner's choices after they propose or accept a redesign.
It supplies definitions and evidence rules; it supplies no preferred solution for the chosen workflow.
If a learner explicitly requests a definition earlier, answer briefly and return to the pending question.
Do not withhold a requested explanation to preserve a reveal.

## What a workflow can establish

A workflow shows how people complete a particular piece of work and exchange the information it needs.
Record separately who performs an action, receives information, makes a decision and directly supervises whom.
One person may do several of these things; involvement in the workflow does not establish a reporting relationship.
The workflow offers evidence about particular structural arrangements, not a complete organisation chart.
Apply the interpretations below only to the learner's recorded before-and-after choices.
Retain “unchanged” and “unknown” as valid conclusions. Do not manufacture a change to cover a syllabus term.

## Decision authority and centralisation

Centralisation concerns where authority to make a decision is concentrated.
Decentralisation moves some authority towards lower levels or more distributed units.
Always name the decision and the comparison: who could decide before, and who can decide now?
Authority to collect information, recommend an action, execute a decision and authorise it may belong to different people.
Giving someone access to a shared record establishes information access; it does not by itself establish authority.
Moving work to another location or tool does not by itself change the location of decision authority.
A policy can retain central authority over some decisions while allowing local decisions within stated boundaries.
Describe such a proposal as selective or bounded delegation, rather than classifying the whole organisation.
If the learner retains the same decision maker, say authority remains unchanged for that decision.
If the learner moves a decision upwards, recognise the increase in centralisation without treating it as an error.
Local speed, capability, consistency and cross-unit consequences are considerations; neither direction is universally better.

Source: OpenStax, Introduction to Business 2e, §7.5, paragraphs defining centralisation and decentralisation and discussing their benefits, risks and contextual factors.
Read: https://openstax.org/books/introduction-business-2e/pages/7-5-degree-of-centralization

## Standardisation, formalisation and discretion

Standardisation establishes common requirements for how work is performed, what it produces or the skills used to perform it.
The facilitator should identify what, specifically, the learner wants to keep consistent across cases.
Common procedures are process standardisation; common required results are output standardisation.
Shared professional preparation can standardise skills while leaving considerable judgement in individual cases.
Do not infer that a common result requires an identical procedure.

Formalisation concerns how explicitly policies, responsibilities and procedures are specified, often in written rules.
A documented rule or role definition supports a claim of greater formalisation in that part of the workflow.
Writing down a learner's description during this exercise does not itself formalise the actual organisation.
The learner must propose that the organisation adopt the rule or record.
Formalisation can support standardisation, but the terms describe different aspects of the design.

Discretion is the room an authorised person has to exercise judgement within the arrangement.
An exception boundary can make that room explicit. Record who may depart from the routine and under what condition.
Do not assume every added rule eliminates discretion or every removed rule creates useful discretion.
A proposal may specify a common required result while allowing different ways to achieve it.

Sources: Henry Mintzberg, “A Typology of Organizational Structure,” printed p. 70, coordination mechanisms; Table 3-1, printed pp. 72-73, behaviour formalisation, training and planning/control systems.

For the explicit-documentation definition: Principles of Management, §7.3, “Formalization,” in the BCcampus-hosted open textbook edition.

## Span of control and supervision

Span of control is the number of employees a particular manager directly supervises.
Count direct reports to the named manager before and after. Mark either count unknown when it has not been established.
Count people, rather than the number of requests, approvals, messages, tasks or workflow participants.
If reporting relationships stay the same, fewer approvals can reduce approval workload while span remains unchanged.
Removing a manager from an approval sequence does not establish that their post or management level has disappeared.
More direct reports means a wider span; fewer means a narrower span, for that manager.
Do not infer a flatter organisation from a shorter process without evidence about reporting levels.
If reporting is shared, distinguish the line manager from project or professional supervision and retain any ambiguity.
Task complexity, employee capability and the need for support affect how demanding supervision is.
There is no ideal number to prescribe from this exercise. A proposed wider span requires a separate capacity judgement.
Less routine approval work may affect how many people a manager could support, but the actual span changes only when their direct reports change. Describe the possible capacity effect as a hypothesis to test.

Source: OpenStax, Introduction to Business, §7.4, “Managerial Hierarchy” and “Span of Control,” including Table 7.2 and the discussion of task complexity.
Read: https://openstax.org/books/introduction-business/pages/7-4-authority-establishing-organizational-relationships

## Coordination and specialisation

Coordination concerns how interdependent contributions are brought together to complete the work.
Identify the required information, its sender and recipient, and how the recipient knows they can proceed.
A shared record, direct discussion, an agreed procedure or supervision can each contribute to coordination.
Claim improved coordination only as an intended effect until the proposed arrangement has been tried.
Specialisation concerns the division of work into roles or units with particular responsibilities or expertise.
Changing who performs a task may change that division; adding a new communication channel need not do so.
Combining tasks may reduce handovers while creating a broader job and new skill requirements.

Source: OpenStax, Principles of Management, §10.1, “Bureaucracy,” “Specialization” and “Command-and-Control.”
Read: https://openstax.org/books/principles-management/pages/10-1-organizational-structures-and-design
Mintzberg's printed p. 70 also distinguishes direct supervision, mutual adjustment and several forms of standardisation as coordination mechanisms.

## How to make the reveal useful

First show the learner's current workflow and proposed workflow in ordinary sentences or arrows.
Then explain the terms supported by their choices, linking each explanation to the relevant change or retained arrangement.
For each core topic, conclude “changed,” “unchanged” or “not established,” with the reason in plain language.
Never announce that the learner decentralised simply because the workflow became faster or had fewer steps.
Never announce a changed span without changed direct-report evidence.
State one intended benefit and one remaining question, using the learner's own priorities where supplied.
Do not promise shorter delays, fewer errors or greater capacity without a comparison or test.
Do not introduce a complete redesign while explaining the concepts.
Use a lateral-thinking method name only when a recorded learner move supports it; an ordinary process improvement is a valid outcome.
If the learner ends early, explain only what the available record supports and leave the remaining fields unresolved.

## Record for the following exercise

Supply a short, copyable record using confirmed answers. Preserve these distinctions:

- The workflow's start, intended outcome and current sequence are known or explicitly missing.
- The learner's proposed sequence is an accepted proposal, rather than a tested implementation.
- Each recorded decision has a named decision maker and an authority boundary, or an explicit unknown.
- Required information has a recorded source and recipient, or an explicit unknown.
- The proposed common rule and allowed exception are recorded in the learner's terms.
- The named manager's direct-report count before and after is stated, unchanged or unknown.
- The intended improvement and any check the learner proposed are separate from demonstrated results.
- Unresolved assumptions and a second connected workflow, if selected, are carried forward without invention.

Do not recommend agents, automation, integrations or technical permissions in this record.
The later exercise can make those choices using the confirmed workflow and its unresolved questions.

## Source use

The explanations above are concise paraphrases and classroom interpretation rules. Use the linked textbook sections to recheck definitions when revising the exercise.
The evidence rules limit what may be concluded from this exercise; they are not empirical findings that every redesign will succeed.
No browsing is required to run the session. The linked sections allow the teacher to inspect the underlying definitions later.
